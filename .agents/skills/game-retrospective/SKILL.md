# Game retrospective

Read [CHARTER](../../../studio/CHARTER.md), [WORKFLOW](../../../studio/WORKFLOW.md), and [TEAM](../../../studio/TEAM.md), then the relevant game brief, decisions, QA, release record, and source opportunity. Use existing records before requesting anything from the owner.

Compare the original hypothesis, scope, time and cash budget, actual artifacts, play feedback, and available commercial results. Keep observed numbers, reported numbers, estimates, and unknowns distinct. Revenue requires a defined period, currency, source, and gross or net basis; do not invent sales from reviews or turn incomplete analytics into zero results.

Ask the `market-analyst` for a bounded performance or competitor comparison when data warrants it. Use the `qa-playtester` to examine recurring quality issues and the `game-designer` to interpret documented player friction. Assign separate notes; the producer integrates conclusions and owns shared status.

Identify a few decisions that changed the outcome, including evidence against the original idea. Separate product appeal, discovery, pricing, execution, and missing information. A single release does not prove a market rule, and a game the owner enjoyed is not evidence of broad demand.

Write a dated retrospective alongside the game's records or in the relevant research run. Include what was expected, what happened, the evidence, what remains unknown, and a concrete next experiment or maintenance action. Recommend continue, patch, reposition, or park within the available budget. Record a material product decision instead of silently changing scope.

Update opportunity status and decision reasons to preserve learning. Keep rejected or parked ideas retrievable. Propose changes to studio guidance only when the evidence supports a reusable improvement; avoid adding permanent ceremony for a one-off failure. Do not create a recurring monitor unless the user requested one.
