# Small premium puzzle and hidden-object evidence

Research date/access date: **2026-09-11**. Assigned analyst artifact; the producer owns shared status, the combined ranking, current Steam snapshots and financial scenarios. This is research, with no game selection or budget authorization.

## Finding

**One-scene hidden-object games are the closest technical fit to a few-day build, but the evidence does not establish that a new entrant can reliably earn NOK 20,000 profit.** There is direct evidence of an incumbent earning a living from a portfolio, alongside free entry products, paid extras, bundles, continuing updates and a warning from that developer about saturation. That is a less passive business than one isolated illustration uploaded to Steam.

**A small spatial puzzle with one repeatable rule is the better differentiated experiment if the owner allows approximately a week rather than a strict weekend.** Its risk is designing enough enjoyable puzzles. A cozy cleaning/sorting anthology, unrestricted building toy or tiny management game should not be treated as a few-day product merely because the player controls are simple.

The inspected evidence establishes paid demand for small relaxing games, not a rising 2026 trend. Trend stage for this bounded scan: **unverified**. Fun, production speed and demand for our proposed version remain untested.

### What the people making these games actually reported

1. **Travellin Cats portfolio: income exists, but the amount is undisclosed.** In the [developer's Steam announcements](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2313250/allnews/), the 2026-02-24 rebranding post says the games are the developer's main income and describes perceived market oversaturation. A 2024-12-10 retrospective reports 25 levels/2,500 cats released in 2024, free collaborations, ports and updates. Later posts describe framework upgrades and accessibility fixes. These are portfolio and workload statements, not per-title paid units or profit. The same announcement feed advertised Germany at USD 0.99/EUR 0.99 before a 10% launch discount in October 2024. The rebranding dispute is reported by one party; no independent legal conclusion is drawn.

2. **Cats Organized Neatly: design and polishing are material work.** In the [GameMaker developer interview](https://gamemaker.io/en/blog/cats-organized-neatly-interview), published **2022-12-07**, DU&I explain that limited programming led them toward simple games; another developer helped with implementation. The level designer acknowledges an uneven difficulty curve and trial-and-error late puzzles. Their advice is to expect finishing to take twice the initial estimate and polishing to take as long as the rough playable version. This is unusually relevant to a non-coder using assistance, but gives no actual project-hour total or financial return.

3. **A Little to the Left: a weekend prototype did not imply a weekend product.** The [developer interview in Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-methodical-catharsis-of-tidying-in-a-little-to-the-left), published **2022-02-15**, reports a roughly 48-hour prototype followed by a month of work on a sharable slice. The developers explain how saves, feedback, controller support, localization, settings and refining puzzles expanded the work. The [publisher's announcement](https://wearesecretmode.com/news/a-little-to-the-left-celebrates-500-000-sales), published/event **2023-05-17**, reports **500,000 copies sold in six months** across the PC/Switch release context. No per-platform split, realized selling price, net receipts, profit or production cost is supplied. The [developer press kit](https://www.maxinferno.com/press/) identifies the original illustration/design and programming/design duo and a third team member joining in 2023; its puzzle-count copy varies, so it is not used as an exact inventory.

4. **SUMMERHOUSE: substantial sales, eight months, experienced creator.** The [original GameDiscoverCo interview](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/summerhouse-and-the-rise-of-the-chill), published **2024-03-27**, reports **120,000 Steam units** by then following the 2024-03-08 launch, a roughly USD 5 price and **eight months of development**. The creator previously co-developed Islanders. Publisher Future Friends describes creators, media and word of mouth; Steam New & Trending/Discovery Queue accounted for about 65% of early page visits. These discovery outcomes belong to that successful launch, not a guaranteed channel for us. Crucially, the article explicitly defines its financial screenshot's “net” as the amount **before Steam pays 70%**. The commonly repeated USD 339,000 figure must not be presented as developer proceeds or profit. We use the reported units and duration, not an income calculation.

No direct paid-unit or revenue disclosure was found for either small paid hidden-object example or Cats Organized Neatly in 15 focused searches and necessary source opens. Search engines prominently surfaced review-derived revenue estimators with mutually inconsistent totals; those were excluded. A retrieved Reddit portfolio milestone was for Minami Lane/Kabuto Park creator Doot, not DU&I; it was not misattributed to Cats Organized Neatly. These gaps lower commercial confidence rather than proving low sales.

## Negative control: readable targets are the product

The [Steam review API query](https://store.steampowered.com/appreviews/1740240?json=1&filter=recent&language=english&review_type=negative&purchase_type=steam&num_per_page=5) returned the five most recent negative, English, Steam-purchase reviews on 2026-09-11. Their posted dates were 2026-06-30, 07-07, 07-31, 09-04 and 09-09; recommendation IDs were 229256663, 229817267, 231719682, 234452139 and 234874295. All complained about target recognizability or inconsistent treatment of shapes as cups. This is a deliberately negative convenience sample, not a frequency estimate across customers. It identifies a concrete failure to test: every target should be recognizable and click boundaries should feel fair.

**Hypothesis:** generated art can accelerate backgrounds, but a credible hidden-object product still needs deliberate target placement, coherent linework, a verified target list and human inspection at ordinary display sizes. A generator producing a pretty crowded scene is not evidence that the hidden objects form a good puzzle. Target ambiguity can erase the apparent labor saving.

Score explanations: spatial fitting has adjacent paid demand and more room for a visible rule difference; level design caps scope at 3. Hidden-object mechanics fit best, but free substitutes, incumbent output and saturation reduce demand confidence for a new paid product. Cleaning inherits paid interest from larger games but requires many bespoke tactile interactions to justify the comparison. Building has the strongest direct small-price sales disclosure but the worst fit to the time budget. Distribution is 2 throughout: plausible channels exist, with no audience or measured conversion for this studio.

**Ranking reversals:** if 12 satisfying spatial puzzles cannot be authored and tested in a day after the mechanic works, hidden-object becomes the better experiment. If a polished original illustration cannot be produced in about one working day, its scope score falls and neither route is a credible few-day commercial project. If Klaus insists on fully explorable 3D, these estimates do not hold. If reused framework/content tools already exist after a first release, later entries could become substantially faster; this is a portfolio hypothesis, not a reason to manufacture a series before one has buyers.

## Positioning, channels and cheapest useful test

**Positioning hypothesis:** a short, attractive evening activity with satisfying completion, readable controls and no time pressure. Avoid presenting “small” as the value proposition; show the exact pleasing interaction in the first ten seconds. Premium pricing assumptions to test are roughly USD 1.99–2.99 for one substantial hidden scene and USD 2.99–4.99 for a coherent small spatial-puzzle collection. These are product experiments, not observed conversion or authorized live prices.

Potential discovery channels are Steam's relevant hidden-object/relaxing/puzzle audiences, small cozy-game creators and short visual before/after clips. Incumbent bundles and cross-promotion are visible but not assets a new studio possesses. Reach from recommendation systems should follow actual interest; it cannot be inserted into the forecast as free marketing. Low text and lightweight hardware could widen access; localization and controller/Deck claims still need real implementation and testing.

**An optional bounded hook:** find camouflaged moths in a night greenhouse; each found moth drifts into a lantern collection, gradually illuminating the illustration. This adds a visible completion payoff while keeping one scene, one click action and a small sprite set. It is a new studio proposal, not a cleared title or globally verified original mechanic. It avoids the scope of hidden-object adventures with narrative inventories and branching puzzles. Existing opportunity hooks were checked; this is not a rewrite of the held packing/stamping concepts.

**Low-cost test before any full product:** one finished scene section with 15 targets, zoom, hint, click response and the collection/lighting payoff. Stop after **6–10 production hours** plus **30–45 minutes of owner review** if the visual promise is still weak. Then test with five people who enjoy observation/cozy games: target at least four understanding it without explanation, no recurring disputes about what counts as a target, and three voluntarily wanting another section. Show a representative full-product scope and USD 1.99–2.99 price separately; stated interest is not a purchase forecast. Recruitment/outbound contact requires the owner's authorization. Until then, prepare the slice and test instructions only after selection is authorized.

Support should be relatively light after stabilization because these routes need no accounts, network service or daily content, but it is not zero: saves, display scaling, input, completion/achievement bugs and broken hints still matter. The incumbent announcements provide actual examples of continuing fixes years after launch. No competitor was personally played during this research. No asset production time, retention, willingness to pay or support-hour rate was measured.

**Return to producer:** integrate the six app IDs with normalized Steam prices/review definitions; compare these route estimates with the horror/arcade/incremental evidence. Favor a tightly capped test over selecting a full hidden-object series. Preserve the distinction between a few days of implementation and a validated commercially worthwhile release.

## Independent challenge to the producer's provisional active-incremental recommendation

Reviewed proposal: a mouse magnet sweeps one tray, toggles polarity to separate valuable scrap from slag, and converts clean hauls into visible upgrades; three materials, ten upgrades, three layers of the same scene and a finite ending. Proposed schedule: one-day feel test, 3–5-day core, 5–8 intensive days for a first commercial candidate. This review is a design/scope critique, not playtest or additional market validation.

**The sorting rule is both the potential hook and the main scope risk.** Simple attraction/collection plus fixed-radius rejection can be bounded. Free physics piles, convincing collisions, attachment chains, material conductivity, deformable piles or a simulation of actual magnetism cannot be casually included. Define the smallest rule in one sentence and demonstrate it before promising the upgrade set. If the player mostly holds a button until clutter disappears, polarity may add busywork rather than an interesting choice. Conversely, if every particle must be classified before moving, it may become a slow puzzle that conflicts with the incremental reward rhythm.

Three material classes, ten upgrades and three layers should be **maximums, not acceptance requirements**. Begin with two classes, attraction/rejection, one sell action and two visibly different upgrades. Ask whether changing polarity causes useful short decisions and whether hauling feels rewarding without permanent numerical growth. Do not use large numbers to rescue a weak interaction. Test one stronger magnet and one wider/alternate collection pattern first; a catalogue of ten small multipliers does not prove depth. Avoid automated upgrades that remove the activity the player came for unless passive automation is deliberately the next reward phase.

**Hidden-object is easier in code, but not necessarily easier for this owner's visual expectations.** A convincing tray with a few reusable, well-lit scrap pieces could be easier to make attractive with Blender/Unreal than a dense, coherent illustration with 100 unambiguous hidden targets. A single illustrated scene may need less programming but substantially more final art direction. A 3D hidden-object alternative adds camera/occlusion/readability work and loses much of the supposed simplicity. Therefore a 3–5-day hidden-object fallback is credible only after a representative finished 15-target section passes the art/readability test; it should not be presented as a guaranteed cheaper route.

**Schedule judgment:** 5–8 intensive days is a plausible experimental allowance, not an evidence-based completion promise for this exact game. The bounded magnet experiment is more useful than spending those days on extensive lore or a large inventory system, but attractive art, input feel, balancing the finite ending, save robustness, upgrade feedback and store material still compete for the same time. The day-one return condition should be an actual playable, visually representative interaction with two material types and two upgrades. Continue only if it feels satisfying and its assets can be reproduced within the remaining budget. If it misses, park or change the interaction; automatically switching genre can create a second unfinished production pipeline.

**What would reverse the recommendation:** if players dislike the polarity decisions but like continuous collection, simplify the sorting requirement; if all appeal depends on expensive particle/physics spectacle, reduce the scope claim; if the first finished illustration is materially faster and more appealing than the first tray, hidden-object becomes the stronger fit. Market evidence for incremental games validates an adjacent audience, not this scrap-sorting rule. The recommendation should therefore be “test this first within a firm cap,” with the revenue target framed as a scenario to assess after market response.
