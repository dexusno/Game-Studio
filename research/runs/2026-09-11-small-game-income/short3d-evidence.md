# Tiny 3D commercial games: evidence and scope challenge

Researcher: market-analysis worker. Accessed 2026-09-11. Assignment: compare single-location horror, tactile work/digging and physics challenges against a few-day build and NOK 20,000 lifetime cash-profit objective. Research only; no project or budget selected. Owner's September 11 request supersedes the earlier FPS ambitions for this comparison. Shared economics and portfolio decisions belong to the producer.

## Finding

Small premium horror and tactile progression games have direct evidence of substantial paid demand. This does **not** establish that a first product can be finished in three to five days or earn the owner's target. The strongest evidence supports **one visually distinctive activity in one compact space**, with an understandable ending or progression arc. Full voxel digging and difficult physics traversal are especially misleading examples of apparent simplicity.

For a 3D route, the most useful experiment is a **fixed-view tactile progression game**, followed closely by **one-room inspection horror** if Klaus prefers scares and is willing to review every authored event. Build one appealing action, one reward change and one satisfying ending before promising a catalog of upgrades or anomalies. The recommendation is a hypothesis, not a selection or income forecast.

The important correction to the usual success stories: the direct publisher interview for *A Game About Digging A Hole* describes **three to four weeks**, pre-existing technology and roughly eight weeks of marketing. The frequently repeated “14 days” is not a dependable from-zero estimate. *The Exit 8* took months. *Buckshot Roulette* had substantial preceding design work and continued full-time development after its initial success. Source details below.

## Proposed routes and consistent scoring

These are original **working hooks**, not cleared commercial titles. Existing candidates were checked for obvious duplication; *False Signal* already covers phone-based horror, so another phone dispatcher is not proposed. No current acceleration has been measured: timing **unverified**, rather than asserting a new rising trend.

Scores use the repository rubric, out of five: demand 20%, distinctiveness 20%, fun potential 20%, scope fit 25%, distribution 15%. All hooks have **low overall confidence** and no playtest. A 2 for fun means plausible and untested. Scores are comparison aids, not predicted return.

| Working hook | Demand | Distinctiveness | Fun | Scope | Discovery | Weighted |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **Magnet Shift**: pull scrap from a fixed salvage tray, preserve valuable finds, cash out for a few visibly stronger tools | 3 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 3 | **2.60** |
| **Mirror Check**: inspect one lift lobby; certify that its mirror agrees with reality before admitting the next passenger | 3 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 3 | **2.60** |
| **Pressure Test**: one battered pressure-testing machine, audible faults, decide whether to bank a payout or attempt a riskier test | 3 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 3 | **2.35** |

**Why the tie still favors Magnet Shift for this owner:** its content can come from a small set of reusable objects and a short progression curve; horror needs authored surprises that Klaus must judge for clarity and effect. Both demand scores come from related paid successes, not measurements of these pitches. Distinctiveness stays 2 because there has been no exhaustive competitor clearance or player comparison. Discovery 3 reflects a legible ten-second before/after or visible contradiction, with a known creator format; no creators have agreed to cover either game. Scope 3 assumes a fixed viewpoint, cosmetic motion instead of unconstrained simulation, and a very small content ceiling. If those constraints undermine the activity, the score must fall.

* **Magnet Shift constraints:** one tray, one input gesture, about six salvage-object types, at most six upgrade purchases, one final recovery. Use authored motion and bounded overlap logic, not hundreds of free rigid bodies, magnetic field simulation or deformable terrain. Risk: if it is simply holding a button until meters fill, there may be no appealing action or premium value. Price to test: $3.99–$4.99, not a chosen store price.
* **Mirror Check constraints:** one environment, no chasing AI, no procedural rooms, no branching lore, a finite set of perhaps 12 carefully composed differences. The mirror can be a deliberately staged view, not a general rendering/portal system. Risk: a one-hour video can reveal every event, and environmental polish plus event QA may consume the entire build budget. Price to test: $2.99–$3.99.
* **Pressure Test constraints:** one machine, three control actions, four consumable tools, short rounds, no opponent rig or multiplayer. Risk: clear risk information and balanced choices are the product; removing those to save time leaves random button pressing. A two-day paper experiment may be cheap, but polished machine feedback and rules testing make three-to-five-day shipping less credible.

The assumption that could reverse this ranking is **proven reusable assets and a fun tested mechanic**. A ready inspection scene plus compelling three-event demo could make horror easier than a disappointing magnet toy. A verified reusable physics controller could make a skill challenge viable later. Without that evidence, rank unconstrained voxel digging and climbing below these routes despite their attractive sales stories.

**One-room horror schedule challenge:** plan on **five to eight agent development days** for a small complete candidate, conditional on an existing usable room kit, simple inspection controls, about 12 finite events, one ending and no custom characters or chase logic. This is an analyst estimate with low confidence, not a demonstrated production duration. Allow about four to six owner hours for judging the scene, spotting unfair clues, full playthroughs and presentation review; store waiting time and cash are separate. A strict three-to-five-day cap favors a slice or an exceptionally constrained fixed-view game. Custom environments, real mirrored-world mechanics, bespoke creature animation, narrative branches, or events that combine freely would push this beyond the proposed envelope. Test three representative events before scheduling the remaining nine.

## Cheapest useful test and owner effort

**Hypothetical envelope, not an allocation:** one agent implementation day for a five-minute *Magnet Shift* slice; one salvage tray, three objects, one upgrade, sound and restart. Klaus spends 20–30 minutes on the first play and a short revision decision. Add two or three target players if available; no outreach has been authorized or performed.

Pass only if players understand the action without spoken instructions, can explain what improved after the upgrade, and voluntarily attempt a second tray. Record whether they enjoy the physical action before introducing extra rewards. Also show a ten-second captured clip and ask viewers to explain the promise. Failure means revise the action once or park it, not add a skill tree. Passing establishes promising feel, not willingness to pay; a demo/store presentation must then test interest at a stated price.

For any commercial continuation, a **three-to-five-day implementation target** requires a frozen feature list and already workable assets. Reserve separate effort for saves/settings, focus/pause, a real packaged playthrough, store art and a trailer. A provisional **four-to-eight owner hours across playtests, presentation and release review** is an estimate to test, not a promise; agent time, calendar store lead time, incremental cash and launch support are separate. No inspected source demonstrates that a first-time creator can reliably obtain NOK 20,000 cash profit with zero follow-up.

## Distribution, refunds and gaps

Steam is the strongest evidenced paid channel in this sample. Creator coverage is plausible because the loops visibly communicate satisfaction, tension or failure; viral coverage remains uncertain, and the source samples skew toward winners. Existing publishers brought distribution advantages. Merely copying a clear genre label is not equivalent to acquiring their audience.

Premium itch is possible: the [official Buckshot Roulette itch page](https://mikeklubnika.itch.io/buckshot-roulette), current 2026-09-11, offers the game for **$2.99 USD minimum**, advertises 15–20-minute runs, and explicitly lacks Steam's multiplayer/online leaderboard. Its ratings and comments are not sales evidence. Several recent purchasers complain about platform-version differences; these are unrepresentative anecdotes but useful support warnings. We found no clean paid-itch unit/revenue denominator for a comparable few-day 3D launch. A free itch demo can test play; free downloads must not be sold to Klaus as evidence of income.

Short games need an honest duration and a satisfying ending. They can be completed within a refund window, but deliberately padding duration would add work and damage the experience. The adverse postmortem above demonstrates returns, without establishing a category-wide refund rate. Store rules and financial sensitivity belong in the producer's economics note.

Material gaps: no audited profits or representative failure cohort; no measurement of our discovery reach; no actual build proving the few-day scope; no price willingness test; no engine/asset integration test; no direct paid-itch profitability sample. Fifteen focused search queries plus source opens were sufficient to establish these limits. Search results that convert review counts into sales were excluded. Current store observations are dated snapshots, not longitudinal growth data. No game selected, no budget changed and no public outreach performed.
