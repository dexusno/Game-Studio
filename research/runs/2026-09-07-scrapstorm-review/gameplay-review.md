# Scrapstorm — independent gameplay and product review

**Reviewer:** fresh game-designer / game-brief analyst. **Date:** 2026-09-07, Europe/Oslo. **Input:** the complete authoritative v0.1 brief, all 17 sections, sample round, design discussion, prototype criteria and source caveats. The independent assessment was written before reading or receiving the commercial review. This corrected version preserves the completed peer challenge-and-response round and incorporates Klaus's subsequent correction about AI development speed, time and the game's purpose.

**My corrected verdict: proceed to a focused playable test, as a recommendation only.** The hook is a plausible seed for a sellable **USD 4.99** arcade action game, and the small rule clarifications below belong in that experiment rather than a separate revision gate. The **current brief does not yet establish an enjoyable, worthwhile premium package or a credible profit case**, but the untested questions justify a cheap experiment, not rejection on an assumed development deadline. Confidence in sellability remains **low**; confidence in testing this interaction next is **medium to high**. USD 4.99 is a conditional price hypothesis supported by adjacent products, not validated willingness to pay for Scrapstorm. I see no current evidence for USD 6.99–9.99; a meaningfully improved package could change that judgment. A lower price would not cure unwanted repetition.

**Correction to my earlier assessment:** I incorrectly treated 6–7 implementation days and 4–6 owner hours as hard ceilings and used human development durations to cast doubt on the AI workflow. Those are earlier planning hypotheses, not limits Klaus imposed. His reported couple-hour Breakout build, supported by the coordinator's local inspection of its substantial current implementation and validation records, is the relevant workflow reference. More development is permissible when useful work improves enjoyment and the prospect of profit. Cheap production or hypothetical small sales alone do not justify making the game. Details and evidence limits appear below; the original peer exchange is retained as history.

The promising product is a small action game whose technique keeps improving. The weak version is a forgettable shotgun with a manual reload, followed by two upgrade cards and a boss. Both fit the prose. A consistent rhythm can be worth mastering; continual tactical novelty is not a requirement for enjoyment. There is no executable or human observation that tells us which version we have. The local portfolio command reports no scaffolded game; this is a desk review, not a playtest or an engineer's implementation estimate.

## What could make someone buy, enjoy, replay or skip it

- **Buy:** the same six visible objects changing from safety to danger is understandable in a short clip. Throwing away a full ring is a stronger image than another generic arena upgrade screen. The little salvage robot could supply warmth and recognition without dialogue or a large world.
- **Enjoy:** a heavy launch, legible exposure and a precise magnetic catch could make a few seconds feel complete. Three hull pips and deterministic blocks permit recoverable mistakes. Finite enemy clears and immediate retry already remove two common sources of dead time.
- **Replay:** improving aim, taking a shorter recovery route and using a smaller volley at the right moment could become learnable technique. A successful return attack caused by repositioning would offer an especially legible moment to repeat. These are hypotheses, not observed pleasures.
- **Recommend:** a player could describe one unusual action and show it working. This requires the throw and recovery to look different from firing ordinary ammunition; texture and a cute character alone will not explain it.
- **Skip:** build-seeking roguelite players may see almost all the systems during their first sitting. Action experts may find two slow threats solvable by circling; less practiced players may spend the fight watching ammo indicators and switching mouse buttons. People drawn by a craft theme may want making or arranging objects, which this game does not contain. That audience mismatch is plausible, not measured.

## Ranked failure modes in the current rules

### 1. Recall is often a default instruction, not a decision — critical

**Sections 4–6, 11 and 17.** When empty, holding global recall has no movement, defensive or offensive cost: shooting is impossible anyway. Harmless returning pieces do not need aiming, and earlier recovery is normally preferable. The natural policy is therefore launch, immediately hold recall, keep moving, fire once enough ammo is home. This could feel satisfying, but the shield image by itself does not turn that sequence into a continuing choice.

There is still a potentially valuable decision **before** launching: keep protection through a dangerous moment, or remove the threat now. Test whether a Spitter actually changes the player's firing time. In the written sample, the player sometimes dodges a shot and then fires; if moving already avoids the slow locked projectile reliably, retaining a shield may be insurance rather than a strategically different action.

**Essential response:** test threat-sensitive launch timing before upgrades. Preserve full movement and reliable global recovery initially. Adding a radius, slowdown, cooldown or reload minigame would manufacture friction without proving better decisions. If players report unwanted repetition, stop learning and see no reason to return, identify a specific testable improvement before continuing; hold if no credible next improvement justifies its actual cost. Retain harmless recall if people enjoy mastering its timing and voluntarily replay after successful clears, even without elaborate return geometry. A new tactical choice on every volley is not required.

### 2. Partial volleys may be accidental timing, or the only efficient policy — high

**Sections 4–6, 8, 11 and 17.** The player cannot deliberately keep four pieces while firing two. They must manipulate when pieces arrive, release recall, then press fire. This is an indirect resource choice with execution overhead.

There is a real possible opportunity: pieces that hit a nearby target return sooner than pieces that miss and travel seven units. At a simplified three-unit impact distance, outbound plus return takes about **3/14 + 3/10 = 0.51 seconds**; at maximum range it is **7/14 + 7/10 = 1.2 seconds**, assuming a stationary player, instantaneous state changes and immediate recall. That can create a readable smaller volley. Conversely, when pieces land at similar distances, the partial window nearly disappears. Actual launch origins, enemy widths, collision timing and player motion are unspecified, so these are arithmetic examples, not balance measurements.

Both enemies have two health. Firing the first two pieces that arrive may therefore become the repeatable optimal attack, with the other four rarely worth waiting for. Or all six may arrive too close together for intentional partial use. Either outcome undermines a prominent selling point.

**Essential response:** observe whether players can predict a useful partial recovery and explain why they took it. Include one near-hit/far-miss arrangement and one clustered landing arrangement. Do not count empty misclicks or random button timing as meaningful variation. If partial volleys add little, stop selling them as a pillar; do not add a separate ammo-selection interface to rescue the claim.

### 3. Return Teeth could repair the loop, or merely double cheap damage — high

**Sections 5, 6, 8, 11 and 17.** Damaging returns offer the strongest additional action: throw to one place, move to another, draw a line through enemies. My hypothesis is that this has more potential than harmless collection as a commercial centerpiece. It is not established.

The exact geometry matters. Baseline scrap lands on its first enemy contact, often on the enemy's near side. Simply returning to the original player position will usually pull it away from the target, not through a crowd. A meaningful second line needs an enemy crossing it, a miss beyond an enemy, or deliberate repositioning. Lance Rails may enable the attractive examples far more reliably than the base weapon.

There is also a dangerous shortcut: if a piece remains touching the enemy it just hit, return damage may apply almost immediately. With two-health enemies, one outbound hit plus one contact-return hit becomes an easy kill. More retries could then reflect higher damage rather than enjoyable path planning. Slower return speed alone does not remove that confound.

**Essential response:** test the toggle from the beginning, using the brief's one return hit per launched piece/cycle. Agree with the engineer on what constitutes a new crossing rather than overlapping contact. Watch whether players reposition to create hits, rather than merely holding recall on impact. Before promoting it, separate path pleasure from extra power with a focused kill-rate tuning comparison if needed. If return attacks supply the distinct enjoyable action, promote them and reduce/replace the card pool; do not keep the best interaction behind an offer. That is a material core revision to take through the producer.

### 4. Shield maintenance can remove danger, and kiting can remove choices — high

**Sections 5, 7, 8 and 13.** A Biter is roughly half as fast as the player. An empty rectangle and slow locked shots invite circling, grouping and firing backward. Grouping is useful technique; it becomes a problem if the same route solves every pack.

Holding recall may also recover a blocked piece soon after its two-diameter ejection. Block grace and Biter pushback could create near-continuous protection in low-pressure encounters. No passive damage means this does **not** win by itself, which is good. But “stay safe indefinitely, briefly fire into a close target, recover immediately” may be both safest and fast enough. One piece already blocks an ordinary hit, so the marginal value of retaining pieces two through six depends on actual overlapping pressure.

**Essential response:** explicitly try stationary recall/blocking and constant perimeter movement during the experiment. Use the already planned split entries and one staggered Spitter to challenge a repeated route. Pressure should make at least two launch/recovery timings useful while remaining avoidable when empty. Do not solve this by filling every escape lane, increasing health, adding permanent ammo loss or restoring damage-on-block.

### 5. The premium replay promise currently falls between two audiences — high

**Sections 2–3, 7–8 and 13–15.** Six nonstacking definitions yield at most **14 unordered final pairs**, excluding Lance + Scatter. There are only two decision events per run. Different acquisition order may change the second encounter, but 14 pairs are not 14 play styles. Grounding Guard and Close Coil mostly change safety/recovery efficiency; Launch Treads adds a modest short boost. The pitch's larger behavior changes concentrate in Lance, Scatter and Return Teeth.

Roguelite buyers may want build discovery; arcade buyers may accept few modifiers if the action has a substantial skill ceiling. Neither audience is satisfied by counting combinations. No permanent power grind is a strength, but the first clear also resolves the story, leaving only records and a cuttable harder preset. Finishing hull has only three successful values. A few easy clears could exhaust both visible progression and meaningful learning.

**Essential response:** foreground an action/mastery promise and test that it is true. Start with the two early choices, and check whether each retained upgrade changes useful execution or tactics in the next encounter. Replace an indistinct upgrade rather than keeping it for the count. If the loop works but discovery runs out, additional behavioral upgrades, enemies or encounters are legitimate next work: compare their actual cost with the improvement in replay and value. Preserve fair comparable record conditions when judging improvement; random offers and entry directions can otherwise make best time feel like an easier roll. A repeatable fixed practice setup is one possible experiment, not an online leaderboard commitment.

### 6. The sample finale's time is not explained by its mechanics — medium to high

**Sections 7, 13 and 17.** A 24-health boss requires only four perfect six-damage volleys before Return Teeth. At the simplified maximum-distance 1.2-second cycle, that is on the order of seconds of uninterrupted attack, not the sample's roughly 110-second finale. Misses, movement and escorts can extend it, but those costs are not specified. This is not evidence that the actual boss will be too short; it is evidence that the written timing lacks support.

**Essential response:** let an exciting short finale be short. Give it a compact combination of the existing shooting tell and capped escorts, and measure it. If a new behavioral problem is needed, prefer one clearly telegraphed remix over a larger health pool or longer waits. Cut the third ordinary encounter if it only rehearses solved play. A five-minute target should never become five minutes of compulsory padding.

### 7. State tracking and button arbitration can consume the attention meant for action — medium to high

**Sections 5, 10 and 12.** The player watches threats, aim, hull, held count, loose scrap and return paths while controlling movement plus two separate action buttons. Six orbiting pieces cannot show all states through appearance alone at every resolution. While recall is held, the brief silently discards a fire press. That conflicts with the exciting partial-volley moment: a player can see the opportunity, press throw, and get nothing because the other trigger remains slightly held.

**Essential response:** test both control paths early enough to affect the rules. Keep strong held/empty feedback, distinct hull/block sounds, quiet background detail and no dangerous overlap in audiovisual cues. If ignored throws occur, test **throw ends recall and launches the currently held pieces**, requiring a fresh recall press to resume. This changes one arbitration rule; it is an experiment, not an instruction to build input automation. Remapping, focus loss, pause and workable menus remain part of a complete paid product.

### 8. Presentation can either explain the mechanism or imply a different game — medium

**Sections 1, 3, 6 and 9–10, 14–16.** The toy-junk direction gives Pip personality and supports clattering collection. Material, lighting, animation, sound and UI are concrete work items whose cost should be measured in this AI workflow; their presence does not imply slow production. GNOG, Wilmot and WALL-E are mood references, not action-demand validation or an asset-production plan.

**Essential response:** one original, legible bot; recognizable enemy silhouettes; consistent scrap outlines; an attack/block/return/hull sound vocabulary. Show the throw and consequence in the first clip. Keep texture and tiny cosmetic differences subordinate to state clarity. Do not imply crafting, custom robot building or object collection that the product does not offer. The upcycling connection can remain inspiration after any trend fades.

There is also a concrete naming issue, supplied during the peer stage and checked on Steam: [a different Scrapstorm by Pixiliated](https://store.steampowered.com/app/5122440/Scrapstorm/) is already listed as coming soon in the bullet-heaven/roguelite neighborhood. Recommend another working title before public branding. This is a search/discovery collision, not a legal finding or a reason to discard the mechanic.

## AI production, enjoyment and profitability

The prototype rules use ordinary 2D collision and six pieces with explicit states; they do not require a new physics engine. Klaus reports that this studio made its Breakout game in a couple of hours during an evening. The coordinator inspected its local README and validation record: the current NEON BREACH beta documents 20 campaign stages across four sectors, 12 upgrades, nine pickups, endless mode, code-authored geometry/audio, saved records/settings, simulation checks, rendered checks, Shipping packaging and native interaction across iterations. This is relevant evidence that this AI workflow can deliver considerably more than a bare mechanic. It does **not** establish that every current feature or check was completed in those first hours, nor did this review rerun them. [Local evidence and attribution](gameplay-evidence/source-notes.md).

The earlier **6–7 days and 4–6 owner hours are adjustable planning hypotheses**, not user-imposed maximums or conditions for continuing. The one-day experiment was also an estimate, not a deadline. Human-team development durations are excluded from my AI effort assessment. Breakout is a better starting reference, although the time for Scrapstorm's particular interaction, polish and iteration is still unmeasured.

The useful next estimate concerns actual incremental work: piece state transitions and collisions; input arbitration; two enemy behaviors; alternate return-hit rules; attack/block/hull feedback; then the selected encounter and upgrade package. A paid version also needs readable presentation, audio, menus, settings/records, both promised control paths and verified packaging. These are a checklist of work to measure, not evidence that it must take a long time. Reuse from the demonstrated workflow may reduce that work. Return-damage changes can affect enemy and upgrade tuning, so measure the resulting iteration instead of assigning a human-team multiplier.

If the core is enjoyable but the first package is too thin, adding a distinct enemy behavior, meaningful upgrade choices, an arena mechanic or a stronger difficulty progression can be worthwhile. The right content budget is the work that makes a desirable product at acceptable actual cost. Removing charm or replay systems just to meet an invented quota could make profitability less likely. Conversely, the ability to generate content quickly is no reason to keep redundant content or assume unlimited free iteration. Material changes to the promise should still go through the producer; this report authorizes no implementation.

Historical developer disclosures remain useful **only for paid-unit, price and discovery lessons here**, not AI schedule estimates:

- The **Swarmlake developer** reports 21,757 units sold for USD 14,192 gross as of September 30, 2018, before listed deductions. It initially sold at USD 0.99 with promotions, despite its current USD 4.99 list price. The lesson is that units and current list price cannot reconstruct receipts. These historical reported results do not establish its profitability or Scrapstorm's demand. [2018 developer paper, section 6 / PDF page 19](https://www.cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/publications/2018/GRIESHOFER-2018-GOS/GRIESHOFER-2018-GOS-thesis.pdf).
- **SNKRX's** July 9, 2021 entry reports 80,781 units sold and substantial creator exposure. That is reported commercial demand for an adjacent game with different depth and discovery. It supplies neither a sales prediction nor a required content count for Scrapstorm. [Post-release log](https://a327ex.com/posts/snkrx_log).

The objective is an enjoyable game with a credible route to profit. For each useful increment, record elapsed active AI work, actual marginal AI/tool/asset charges and owner attention separately; distinguish included subscription usage from new cash expense. Use that evidence to revise the remaining work and support allowance. A business comparison should use plausible receipts per paid copy after applicable deductions, discounts and refunds, against actual incremental cash costs, an explicit treatment of shared costs, and owner time reported separately or valued transparently. Demand scenarios require evidence and sensitivity checks; review totals and a hypothetical few hundred sales do not establish either demand or success. Faster coding improves cost feasibility but cannot establish wanted repeat play, purchase appeal or profit.

## Cheapest next evidence that could change my verdict

**Recommendation only:** start with the brief's three-minute arena comparison. Treat its earlier one-day allocation as an unverified planning estimate and measure the actual AI work; no minimum day or completion deadline is implied. Use six conserved pieces, the two enemies, clear attack/block/hull/recovery cues, death and immediate restart. Leave upgrades and broader content out of this initial comparison so the core rule can be assessed. Include the small paper-rule clarifications in the experiment: the engineer should confirm deterministic piece states, collision contact handling for a return hit, input arbitration and a quick tunable return-damage toggle. These are implementation dependencies, not a separate approval gate.

1. Observe the base rule against a Biter, then a readable mixed Biter/Spitter pack. Include one spread of near-hit and far-miss scrap. A player must be able to explain a block versus hull hit and choose a launch moment without being coached into the sample sequence.
2. Give roughly equal brief exposure to harmless and damaging returns, reversing order between available players. Record deliberate target grouping, launch timing changes, partial throws, useful return-path repositioning, failed inputs and which actions feel like work. Do not treat a faster clear with extra damage as proof of a better choice. Test the simple passive/circling policies separately.
3. Keep the brief's directional five-player checks: four explain protection/ammunition; three deliberately vary a throw/recovery choice; three voluntarily retry. Record **replay after a successful clear separately from retry after defeat**, plus the reason and any improved technique. A technically correct repeat performed to satisfy the observer does not count as voluntary replay. Failing the tactical-variety count is diagnostic, not an automatic rejection when post-clear replay and improving execution are strong. This sample cannot prove retention or commercial demand.
4. If the core earns wanted play, test the proposed content progression and a later short return session with the same players. Present the actual proposed package honestly: the current baseline is one arena, four encounters, two choices, local records and USD 4.99. Ask what remains worth returning for and what would stop a purchase. If enjoyment is strong but value/replay is thin, test one specific content improvement and measure its cost; do not automatically reject a larger useful package. Stated intent is still weaker than an actual purchase; no outside outreach or public test is being conducted here.

**Short playtest question:** “Which moment made you change your plan, and which action would you want to do again?”

Approximately **60–90 minutes of observation for five short two-variant sessions** is an optional planning estimate if players are already available, not an owner-time ceiling or a requirement that Klaus observe every session. Record recruitment, observation, later return sessions and owner decisions as actual separate work. Player observation and paid-product verification remain necessary evidence; their cost should be counted without presuming that they are slow or free.

**Recommend continuing toward a paid package** when players enjoy mastering the exposure/recovery interaction and return after successful clears and initial novelty, and the emerging package has a credible value and profit case using observed AI costs and owner time. This does not require advance certainty of sales. **Revise or hold** when play remains unwanted and no specific improvement has a credible chance of earning enjoyment and paid value at an acceptable cost, or when the cost/demand case remains unattractive after useful evidence. Needing more meaningful enemy/build content is a scope decision, not an automatic failure. Do not promote Return Teeth merely because more damage raises clear rate. A three-minute test can justify further work; it cannot establish broad enjoyment or profitability by itself.

## Original peer challenge record — historical

The independent draft and its verdict were sent to the coordinator before either specialist read the other's work. After the coordinator confirmed both drafts, I read the complete commercial review and exchanged the following direct messages with the commercial analyst on September 7. Each sent a substantive challenge and answered the other's challenge; no agreement was required. This records the original exchange. Its budget framing was subsequently superseded by Klaus's correction, as recorded below.

- **My challenge to the commercial reviewer:** the initial allowance for an unusually satisfying one-clear object, and a $2.99–4.99 testing range, could become an escape from the current brief's unproved replay.
