**Coordinator recommendation after Klaus's correction: proceed to a short playable core test, with the specific rule clarifications below included in its specification.** This is a recommendation, not implementation authorization. The concept has a credible opportunity to become enjoyable and profitable with this studio's AI workflow. Test that opportunity, then choose the content and finishing scope that gives buyers a worthwhile game at an acceptable actual cost. A few hundred sales are worthwhile only if their realized proceeds justify those costs and owner effort; a quick build is not itself a reason to make the game.

**Correction to the initial review:** Klaus has set **no hard development-time limit**. The earlier 6–7 days and 4–6 owner hours were planning hypotheses; treating them as ceilings was our error. Human teams' development durations are not a valid estimate of this AI coding workflow. Klaus's couple-hour Breakout result and its documented implementation are the relevant local starting evidence. This correction removes the schedule-based objection and the assumption that the product must stay extremely small. It does not establish Scrapstorm's fun or sales in advance.

The strongest likely reason to buy is a readable, physical moment: an appealing little robot throws away its protection, escapes and snaps the same objects home. The strongest reason to skip is that this moment becomes a solved reload routine, with little left to learn after two upgrades and a first clear. There is **no playable build, human fun assessment, purchase-intent result or measured finishing duration**. These conclusions concern plausibility, not observed enjoyment or promised commercial success.

## The changes that matter most

| Priority and brief sections | Why the current design could fail | Essential response and scope effect |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The repeated action — §§4–6, 11, 17** | Immediate global recall is normally useful whenever empty. Partial volleys depend on pieces arriving separately and on a player releasing recall before firing. These may be deliberate choices, an awkward input exercise, or a routine two-piece shot against two-health enemies. | Observe launch timing, aim/grouping and intentional partial shots. A regular rhythm is acceptable if technique improves and players want another clear. Keep the current reliable recall initially. If firing while recall is held repeatedly produces unwanted ignored input, test one rule change: throw ends recall and launches held pieces; a fresh recall press resumes it. No new attack-selection interface. |
| **2. Return damage — §§5, 8, 11, 17** | The most convincing written sequence repeatedly relies on Return Teeth, often with Lance. Slower damaging recall may create useful attack routes, or simply grant more damage and easier kills. Pieces landing on first contact might hit the same overlapping enemy immediately when return starts. | Compare harmless and damaging returns early. Require a new return crossing to distinguish route play from contact damage; retain one return hit per launched piece/cycle. Watch repositioning and account for easier kills before judging preference. Promote return damage only if the evidence supports it. This affects balance and the upgrade pool, so resolve it before expanding content. |
| **3. Safe repetitive play — §§5, 7–8, 13** | Slow Biters, locked shots, a clear rectangle and rapidly recycled blocked pieces may permit safe waiting or perimeter kiting. Never attacking cannot win, but nearly costless waiting between attacks can erase the intended exposure. | Deliberately test stationary recall, circling and close-range fire/recall. Use existing entry patterns and Spitter timing first. Adjust recovery or pressure only if a dominant strategy is observed; preserve avoidability while empty. No passive block damage, permanent ammo loss or extra enemy inventory as an automatic repair. |
| **4. Reasons to replay — §§7–8, 12–15** | Two choices from six upgrades give at most **14 legal unordered final pairs**, not 14 distinct play styles. Two ordinary enemy types may stop asking new questions quickly. One clear resolves the story, and the harder preset is cuttable. | Position the product around short arcade mastery with light build variation. Retain cards that visibly change decisions; cut redundant ones and repetitive encounters. Test replay after a successful clear and a later return session. Keep hard mode only if it improves fair mastery; cutting it is not harmless when it supplies the main remaining goal. |
| **5. Actual production economics — §§2, 9–10, 12, 16** | Coding speed alone does not tell us whether players want the result, but the earlier review had no valid basis for forecasting slow AI delivery. There is no hard time ceiling. The outstanding commercial question is the actual cost of making a sufficiently good package relative to obtainable paid demand. | Use measured AI delivery and incremental charges, asset needs, owner attention and support expectations. Add a meaningful threat, upgrade or arena feature if its value justifies that cost. Keep responsive controls and coherent feedback; remove low-value work. Additional development is worthwhile when it improves the enjoyment/profitability case, not automatically a reason to hold. |
| **6. Name and audience — §§1–3, 9–10, 15** | Another upcoming Steam action roguelite is already called Scrapstorm. A toy-junk image may also imply crafting or gentle collection to viewers who would not choose this action game. | **Replace the working title before public branding.** Show aiming, danger and recovery immediately; use toy materials to make combat recognizable. This is a cheap positioning correction, not a reason to abandon the mechanic. [Existing Steam listing](https://store.steampowered.com/app/5122440/Scrapstorm/). |

Two further paper-spec corrections cost little:

- **Four upgrades can break the offer rules (§§8, 16).** If Lance and Scatter both remain, excluding every previously shown card may leave only one legal second option. Example: first show Lance + Return, choose Lance; among Scatter + Coil, only Coil is compatible. A four-card pool needs a compatible composition or permission to reoffer a declined card. The full six-card pool does not have this failure.
- **The finale's 110-second script is unsupported (§§13, 17).** A 24-health Foreman takes four perfect six-hit outbound volleys. Escorts, misses and movement could extend it, but those costs are unspecified. Tune a satisfying finish and let the time emerge; do not protect the sample clock with inflated health or waiting.

Detailed arithmetic, assumptions and input-integrity checks are in the [coordinator audit](coordinator-evidence/audit.md). Those calculations are not a collision simulation or a playtest.

## What the market evidence supports

Across their snapshots they inspected **12 distinct priced games**, including close recovery mechanics, small premium packages and weak adjacent responses. This is a purposive sample selected to challenge this brief; it is not representative of all games. The two review samples overlap and cannot be combined into independent votes or prevalence estimates.

No human-duration-based estimate fills those gaps.

## Budget, theme and discovery

Keep **AI execution, owner attention, incremental cash, calendar and support** separate. Klaus reports that the Breakout game was made in a couple of hours in an evening. Its current NEON BREACH documentation records 20 campaign stages across four sectors, 12 upgrades, nine pickups, endless play, code-authored geometry/audio, saves, packaged Windows builds and verification across iterations. The coordinator read its README and validation record; no tests were rerun, and the later inventory is not all attributed to the first two hours. This is direct local capability evidence, unlike human-team calendars.

There is **no hard development or owner-time cap**. Estimate new work from this workflow and the next measured increment. The profitable-product case needs obtainable paid demand, realized proceeds after discounts/refunds/store deductions, incremental AI/tool charges, assets and release costs, plus support and owner effort. Existing subscription allocation and extra cash charges should be recorded separately; no human programmer wage is imputed to AI coding. Those inputs are not all known yet, so there is no defensible profit forecast. The commercial review includes explicitly illustrative cost/contribution scenarios to show how measured costs translate into the sales needed for cash break-even; those are not sales estimates. The next evidence should improve the enjoyment and profitability case.

If the core is enjoyable but two enemies and six upgrades leave too little value, evaluate a focused expansion: a threat that changes recovery routes, upgrades with visibly different play, or useful arena geometry. Measure its production cost and player benefit. A more substantial game is allowed; extra inventory without better play is not an economic argument. Details of the correction and Breakout reference are preserved in the coordinator audit.

Steam's current public guidance includes a USD 100 per-product fee, first-few-title waiting requirements, and at least two weeks of public Coming Soon visibility. These are separate from implementation days and depend partly on account state. No store action or fee payment was performed. [Steam Direct](https://partner.steamgames.com/steamdirect), [Coming Soon](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/coming_soon).

Pinterest's source reports **437% growth in global miniature-from-trash text searches, May 2026 versus May 2025**, published August 25. The coordinator rechecked its ordinary public HTML after the web renderer redirected. It establishes historical hobby interest, not September acceleration or demand for this PC game. Keep the upcycling inspiration if it improves identity after the trend fades. [Pinterest report](https://newsroom.pinterest.com/news/2026-pinterest-hobbies-trend-report/).

A clear gameplay clip and eventual playable sample are plausible ways to reach active-action players; neither audience access nor conversion is established. Steam describes purchases and play as important visibility signals; page traffic alone does not establish broader exposure. A searchable name is useful, but a renamed game would still need people to understand and want the action. [Steam visibility documentation](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/visibility).

## The cheapest evidence that would change this recommendation

These are recommendations for a later authorized experiment. No implementation, selection, recruitment, publication or spending was undertaken.

1. **Correct the test specification.** Define return-hit contact handling and current input arbitration. Begin with one arena, six conserved pieces, two enemies, clear event sounds, death and immediate restart. This initial scope isolates the question; it is not a shipping-content ceiling. No upgrade system is needed for this first test.
2. **Try to break the core.** Exercise stationary recall/blocking, perimeter kiting, near-hit/far-miss partial recovery and clustered returns. Observe whether the Spitter changes when someone throws and whether partial shots are intentional. A steady cadence can pass if better aim, routes or execution are rewarding.
3. **Compare harmless and damaging recall.** Reverse variant order between available players. Record useful movement, described enjoyable moments, ignored inputs and kill-rate/pressure differences. Preference for more power alone does not establish the proposed return-path decision.
4. **Keep the brief's directional small-sample criteria:** with five available target players, four explain protection/ammunition, three deliberately vary a relevant decision, and three voluntarily retry. Separately record retries after defeat and replay after a successful clear, reasons for stopping, and whether a later short session reveals further interest. These counts guide design; they do not estimate retention or sales.
5. **If the action passes, choose and test a worthwhile complete proposition.** Start by evaluating the brief's inventory and USD 4.99 hypothesis honestly. If it is too thin, cost a concrete improved package using the AI workflow instead of assuming those counts must stay fixed. Allow “I would skip it” and ask what players would choose instead. Stated intent remains weaker than a purchase. Combine observed enjoyment, price/package response and measured incremental costs into a credible profitability case before committing to the full product.

**Continue** if players understand the consequences, enjoy improving and voluntarily return, and a concrete product scope has a credible profitability case. **Hold or redesign** if the experience remains unenjoyable or plausible obtainable proceeds do not justify its actual costs and owner effort. Needing more useful content or more development time is not an automatic failure. More cards, a longer run or a cheaper sticker should earn their place through better player value and economics.

## What the peer challenge changed

Exactly two fresh specialists reviewed the full brief independently before reading each other's reports. They then exchanged substantive objections and answers directly through the collaboration tools.

- The market analyst challenged the designer's initial suggestion that a comfortable repeated attack cadence should trigger a hold. The designer accepted that a consistent rhythm can work through improving technique and voluntary post-clear replay. Novel tactical decisions on every volley are not required.
- The designer challenged the market analyst's fallback of a cheaper, unusually satisfying one-clear object. The market analyst withdrew the implied $2.99–4.99 recommendation: this exact six-minute combat package has no evidence for that alternative promise. It would require a separately revised product decision.
- Both strengthened the return-damage test around new crossings and comparable challenge, and separated post-clear replay from defeat retries. The coordinator's four-card offer failure and verified name collision were incorporated after the independent stage.
- At that stage, both retained **revise first** and rejected a higher-price or larger-content assumption as a substitute for evidence. The designer was more interested in damaging-return geometry; the market analyst remained open to harmless recall if the actual rhythm earned mastery. Neither variant was chosen by agreement. The time/production reasoning and current recommendations were subsequently corrected below.

Klaus then corrected our production assumptions. Both specialists were asked to revise their own reports using the Breakout precedent, remove invented time caps, and reassess any rejection based on more content taking longer. Their current verdicts appear at the top; the exchange above records the earlier substantive design/price discussion. The coordinator now recommends proceeding to the core test because the remaining objections are testable design/value questions, not a substantiated AI delivery-time barrier.
