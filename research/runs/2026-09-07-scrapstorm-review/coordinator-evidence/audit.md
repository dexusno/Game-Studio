## Mechanical assumptions worth trying to break

| Brief sections | Calculation or rule interaction | Consequence and cheapest check |
|---|---|---|
| 4–6: recovery and exposure | At maximum outbound range, a stationary ideal path is `7 / 14 = 0.5 s` outbound and `7 / 10 = 0.7 s` return. A complete cycle is about **1.2 s** before input/hits/movement change it. Full rectangle diagonal is `sqrt(24² + 14²) / 10 = 2.78 s` at baseline return speed. | Global recall removes searching, but may leave repeated fire/recall alternation. Neither figure proves that recovery feels slow or tactical. Compare ordinary rhythm with deliberate cross-field return routes; count decisions, not button presses. |
| 5: partial volleys | Six pieces launched together into a symmetric empty fan land at nearly equal distances. They may return nearly together unless enemy hits or player movement separate their paths. Enemy health is two, so the proposed two-piece finish requires **both** to connect. | A smaller shot exists in the rules but the player may have almost no useful time to select it, or may repeatedly fire one ineffective piece. Measure attachment spacing and intentional partial finishes. Do not add a new attack button before determining whether the current interaction is usable. |
| 5: protection and recall | Guard grace caps ordinary block consumption at four pieces per second. Six pieces bridge roughly **1.5 s** between the first block and a seventh potential block. The fixed two-unit inward segment of a spent piece takes only **0.2 s**, plus unspecified outward travel and movement. Holding recall costs no speed and returns ejected pieces. | Plausible renewable shield while stationary/slow-kiting; **not a proven invulnerability exploit**. It cannot win without attacking because shielding deals no damage. However, it may trivialize waiting between attacks. Stress-test repeated Biter contact and mixed Spitters while holding recall; establish whether pieces are depleted before recycling. Only adjust ejection/recovery or attack pressure if this actually collapses risk. |
| 5, 11, 17: threat and sample exchange | Biters move at half player speed, projectiles lock aim, there is no compulsory timer, and normal outbound pieces stop after their first hit. | Perimeter kiting and waiting for a safe volley could dominate. Directly aligned enemies may absorb shots intended for enemies behind them; the illustrative opening is not a guaranteed two-kill result. Inspect real trajectories and ask whether a Spitter changed the throw, rather than merely the movement direction. |
| 8: return damage | Return Teeth adds another eligible one-damage hit per launched piece, with slower return. This can greatly increase damage opportunities as well as create a new aiming route. | A more popular return-damage version might simply be easier. Compare route changes, enjoyment descriptions and replay while accounting for kill rate/pressure. Keep its one-return-hit eligibility until the next actual launch; repeated recall toggles must not rearm it. |
| 8, 16: upgrade combinations | Six distinct upgrades have **14 legal unordered final pairs**, `C(6,2) − 1`, after excluding Lance + Scatter. This is a configuration count, not 14 meaningful builds. Exhaustive enumeration found no impossible second offer with all six. | Content variety remains unproven. The count ignores offer probabilities, dominance and similarity of play. First-pick order also affects the early encounter, without creating a new final pair. |
| 8, 16: the four-upgrade cut | If a reduced pool retains Lance, Scatter and two others, the rule excluding **all previously shown** cards can leave only one valid second-offer card. Example: first show Lance + Return, choose Lance; Scatter is incompatible, so only Coil remains. Four of the twelve first-offer/selection branches fail in this example pool. | The cut is not universally safe. Remove the conflicting pair from the smaller pool, or permit a previously declined card to return. Keep two distinct valid choices. This is a paper-spec correction; it needs no game build now. |
| 13, 17: finale length | A 24-health Foreman needs only **four** perfect six-hit outbound volleys without return damage. The scripted finale lasts approximately 110 seconds. Hitbox, movement, escort obstruction and actual hit rate are unspecified. | This is a mismatch in assumptions, not a measured four-second boss. Tune for a worthwhile learned-threat finish and let duration emerge; do not inflate health or force waiting to make the sample clock true. |
| 7–8, 12–13: mastery | Best time rewards aggression; best finishing hull can reward patience. Upgrade offers and entry variation affect difficulty, while the brief does not specify an identical-run retry or comparable record categories. | Good local records can motivate mastery cheaply, but random offers may make a record feel like a lucky draw. A fixed test seed is sufficient for evaluation; seeded score modes or medals are optional product choices, not automatic extra scope. |

Calculations and offer enumeration were run with Python's standard library on 2026-09-07. This verifies arithmetic and offer-rule feasibility only. No simulation of collisions, aiming, enemy AI or fun was performed.

## Production economics — corrected after Klaus's clarification

The initial audit incorrectly treated six to seven days as a stop/re-scope ceiling. **Klaus has set no hard development-time limit or four-to-six-hour owner cap.** Those figures were earlier planning hypotheses. He also correctly challenged using historical human development durations to infer how long our AI coding work should take. That comparison is withdrawn, including its implication that the proposed content must remain tiny to fit an unagreed week.

The relevant local evidence is **NEON BREACH**, from the saved Breakout project. Klaus reports making the game in a couple of hours in an evening. The coordinator read its `README.md` and `Tests/VALIDATION.md` on September 7. The current documented beta 4 has 20 campaign stages across four sectors, 12 upgrades, nine pickups, an endless mode, code-authored geometry/audio, records/settings and a Windows Shipping package. The validation record documents simulation regressions, rendered checks, packaged launches and native interaction across iterations. This shows substantial delivered capability; these checks were not rerun for this review, and later additions are not all assigned to the first evening's hours.

The task tool also located **Build modern Breakout game**. Its turn-content responses were empty, so no active-work duration was reconstructed from task age or timestamps. The couple-hour benchmark is Klaus's first-hand report. The documented artifacts corroborate that the reference was a substantial implemented game, rather than merely a design pitch. No Breakout source or build file was changed.

For Scrapstorm, measure work increments using this actual workflow. Recall rules, enemies, visual/audio feedback, controls and packaging are concrete tasks, not evidence they must consume human-team weeks. Integration and player feedback still need checking, but their duration must be observed instead of assumed slow. The lack of a Scrapstorm build means an exact completion time is unknown; it does **not** establish that six or seven days is optimistic.

The governing test is **an enjoyable product with a credible profitability case**. Record actual incremental AI/tool charges, any assets or commissioning, release costs, support exposure and owner effort; keep allocated subscriptions separate from extra cash payments. Do not impute a human programmer salary to AI execution. Compare those costs with plausible obtainable paid demand and realized per-copy proceeds after discounts, refunds and store deductions. A small number of sales is worthwhile only if that comparison works. There is no adequate input set yet for a numeric profit forecast.

If the action is promising but the present inventory is too thin, consider a useful enemy, upgrade family or arena feature and measure both its AI production cost and its effect on play/value. More development is permitted when it strengthens the case. Hold for persistent unenjoyable play or unattractive actual economics, not merely because a larger inventory or more time is needed. No implementation, spending or external action is authorized by this research correction.

## Whole-product cross-check

Sections 1–3 supply a clear sentence and an appropriate active-action audience, but the roguelite label increases expectations for run variation. Sections 4–6 define a potentially expressive shared resource, with the unverified timing and geometry above. Sections 7–8 make a commendably finite content commitment; two choices, entry variation and a harder preset still need a reason to replay after success.

Sections 9–10 give the robot a purpose and a recognizable toy-scale material language without expensive dialogue. Retain that identity in simple silhouettes, motion and sound. A pleasant junk object by itself does not demonstrate an action game; the eventual opening clip needs a visible attack, exposure and recovery. The proposal already distinguishes threats by more than colour and prioritizes warning sounds, which are useful requirements to preserve when cutting cosmetic detail.

Section 11 and section 17 are vivid test scenarios, not evidence the player can or wants to make those decisions. Sections 12–13 have sensible recovery from defeat and focus loss. The added control load is learning to release recall before a partial throw while aiming and moving, especially with triggers; controller support requires interaction testing, not just binding four axes and two buttons. Sections 14–15 make an honest paid-once promise but do not establish price acceptance or reachable buyers. Section 16 names sensible cuts; the upgrade-pool edge case shows why cuts need interaction checks. The proposed next test correctly prioritizes the core, but its voluntary-retry observation should distinguish retry after failure from replay after a successful clear.
